# Tender Survey

*A visual philosophy*

**Tender Survey** takes the cold instruments of measurement — graticules, hairline rules, concentric rings, specimen grids, index numbers — and uses them on the things that are hardest to measure: nearness, belonging, care. It borrows the look of the survey plate and the naturalist's specimen drawer, then makes them warm. The work should feel like an artefact from an imaginary discipline that treats closeness as a physical quantity, observed patiently and recorded with reverence. Every plate must look meticulously crafted, the product of deep expertise and countless unhurried hours.

**Space and form.** Space is surveyed, not decorated. Compositions sit on an invisible grid of exact margins, and generous paper is left open so that each measured mark reads as deliberate. Forms are perfect and few: the hairline contour, the dot, the circle and the concentric ring, the eight-spoked rosette. Density comes from patient repetition: hundreds of identical marks gathered until they become a texture, a population, a weather. From across a room the eye sees one calm shape; up close it finds thousands of individual observations. Each placement should show the painstaking attention of a master draughtsman.

**Colour and material.** The palette is a single family of warm tones, like dried pigment: paper the colour of unbleached linen, a deep oxblood ink for anything that must carry weight, one cinnabar red kept for the single point of attention, and a quiet ladder of peach and salmon tints that turns quantity into warmth. There are no gradients for effect and no black. Tone is laid down in a few calibrated steps, as a lithographer would, so every shift in warmth means something. Colour carries information; it is never ornament.

**Scale and rhythm.** Meaning comes from contrast of scale. One large, quiet field faces a dense ledger of tiny specimens, and one precisely placed ring answers a thousand scattered points. The rhythm is a slow pulse: long stretches of stillness, then clusters of fine, crowded detail. Repetition is the grammar. The same small figure repeats in rows until the differences between individuals become the story. Weight is always earned through accumulation, never through volume.

**Composition and hierarchy.** Hierarchy moves from field, to cluster, to single mark, to word. Text arrives last and says little: small thin serifs and clinical monospace, used like the annotations on a museum plate. That means coordinates, reference numbers, a plate title, and at most one short phrase that holds the whole piece like a caption holds a photograph. Nothing touches, nothing crowds, and nothing leaves the sheet. Alignment is absolute. The finished work should look impossible to improve: master-level execution in which every hairline, every gap and every letter has been weighed, adjusted, and weighed again.
